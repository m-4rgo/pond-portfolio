Intro


This journal focuses on coat genetics for Equus Aves.

These mutations are caused by genes, and they occur according to probabilities. The mutations that affect coat and feather are separate, meaning that feather genetics cannot affect coat phenotypes and vice versa.
Full genotypes for equus avis are those which list both coat and feather genos, the two sections separated by a "//" or "\\". For example, the full genotype for ID#0025 is: ee/aa/nTo\\ee/aa/toTo/SPLSPL/minMIN/intINT/bubl/BB

This means that she carries heterozygous tobiano on her main body, but homozygous splash and heterozygous tobiano oh her wings. She exhibits two shades of blue, but we only write the one blue colour gene. She exhibits "minimal" feather markings (see "Feather Genetics"), as well as "intermittent" ones. The minimal can be seen on the tail feathers, the intermittent on the inside of the wing.


General

Coat and feather genetics work independently from one another.
This means that yes, it is possible for a coat to be for example, buckskin, and a feather to be palomino.
For coat genes: generally, if a gene is heterozygous (like fleck, for example), it will have minimal phenotypical appearance. If it is homozygous, it will have maximal appearance. Much the same way the splash gene works in horses.
For feather genes: some standard horse coat genotypes can cause patterns that look similar to the fade gene. For example, a buckskin wing would have a palomino base colour, but would also have a black fade pattern, usually extending from the tip of the wing, though this is not always the case (Sometimes it can extend from the base)! The same is true for other coat genes like sooty, and roan.



Coat genetics & mutations

Equus aves use all standard phenotypes and genotypes for normal horses.
Below is a current list of possible mutations in Equus Aves.
Iridescence iri/IRI - the coat glimmers a range of colours.
Chimera chi/CHI - we treat chimera differently, like a recessive gene. The genotype that will be passed on from a chimeric EQA is picked randomly. For a bred EQA to exhibit chimera, BOTH its genotypes must have the chichi gene. In the case that one genotype has a chiCHI gene, this genotype will be the phenotype of the horse. The other genotype will essentially "disappear" as it is overwritten.
E.g. A bay chimera carrier breeds with a black and chestnut chimera. This is EE/Aa/chiCHI x (EE/aa/chichi + ee/aa/chichi). The main base turns out to be EE/Aa/chichi; a bay chimera exhibitor. The second genotype for the EQA turns out to be Ee/aa/chiCHI; a black chimera carrier. The black genotype does not carry the recessive chichi. Therefore, the black genotype overrides the bay genotype, and the foal turns out as a black chimera carrier.
Fleck fle/FLE - the colour of this marking often correlates to wing feather colours. When writing genotypes, we include the colour group of fleck in the feather genotype, if it is not a colour found in the wing itself.

Feather Genetics

Mutations may appear in imports or may be purchased for an existing EQA or prospective foal from the store.
When a gene is expressed both on the keel and wing feathers, it is homozygous. When it is expressed in just one area, it is heterozygous.
Other feathers, like beard and elbow feathers are not regulated with their own genetics; they will be determined by alleles present in the feather genotype. If a gene is homozygous, it must appear on at least 2 feather regions, but does not need to appear on elbow feathers if it appears on wing and keel. If a heterozygous allele is not shown on either wing or keel, it may appear on elbow feathers.

Colour Inheritance

There are two gene types, and they are primary and secondary genes. They refer to the colour groups, primary and secondary colours.
Primary genes are :bulletred:RR, :bulletblue:BB, and :bulletyellow:YY, or in other words, red, blue, and yellow. They could also be called "pure" genes..
When homozygous primary genes are present, the same primary phenotype is expressed (red, yellow, or blue).
When heterozygous primary genes are present (e.g, :bulletyellow:YY + :bulletblue:BB), a secondary phenotype is expressed (orange, green, or purple), in this case it is green. In genotypes they are listed as :bulletorange:YR, :bulletgreen:YB, and :bulletpurple:RB respectively.
When heterozygous primary-secondary genes are present (e.g, :bulletyellow:YY + :bulletpurple:RB), genetic outcome will be a mix of the two. So in this instance, possibilities are RY and BY.
When heterozygous secondary genes are present (e.g, :bulletorange:RY + :bulletgreen:YB), offspring will inherit a mixture. In this case, the possibilities are: RY, RB, YB, YY.
When an EQA has no apparent colour group (i.e, has bare, plain wings), its genotype is 00. This is the limiter gene, and like the chestnut base in horses, is the base gene for all feather colours. It is a dominant gene. It takes the place of one allele on a genotype, so any limited EQAs can only carry one colour allele (R, Y or B). For example, a red carrying, limited EQA would have the genotype of: 0R.



Shades & Saturation

Various shades of the 6 colour groups (red, yellow, blue, green, orange, purple) can be expressed; as many as are visible to the human eye!
They are not controlled by genes (except for the points below) and can vary due to sun-bleaching and seasons. To determine what shade or saturation a foal's coloured feathers should be, look to the parents. Choose the sire's, dam's, or a mix between the two's shade and saturation.
When the "duller" gene is present, vibrant shades will be replaced with dulled down browns and greys. This can be differentiated from the grey gene because shades are dull at birth.
When heterozygous grey (GG) is present in feather genotypes, feather colours will desaturate to their grey value over time.
Black and white are shades, but are "true" shades, and can only be expressed when another gene is present.
The bleach gene (BL/bl) allows true white to be expressed as a feather colour.
White markings such as tobiano and overo can be expressed when the bleach gene is not present.
The burn gene (BU/bu) allows true black to be expressed.
These "true shade" exhibitor genes need only be heterozygous for true white or black to be expressed.
They are both alleles of the same gene. With this in mind, let's look at the following genotypes:
buBL - means that true white can be exhibited, but not true black.
BUbl - means that true black can be exhibited, but not true white.
BUBL - means that both true black and true white can be exhibited.
bubl - means that neither true black nor true white can be exhibited.



Multiple Colour Expression

There are 6 colour groups, but as far as we know, EQAs can express all six in various shades and saturations!
When we write genotypes for EQAs, we do not include a separate gene for every shade of a colour expressed, we simply include one gene for each colour group. This means that the maximum number of feather colour genes that would be written into a genotype would be six.
For example, an EQA that exhibits two solid shades of blue and two shades of orange on a chestnut base, and no true black or white could have the full genotype of: ee/aa\\ee/aa/solSOL/bubl/BB/BY (though there are numerous other possibilities)
When breeding EQAs that have different numbers of colour groups, we sub the existing gene again into the parent with fewer colour groups' genotype.
For example; we are breeding solid red and green with solid yellow. So parent 1's genotype is RR/RB and parent 2's is YY/YY. So the outcomes will be a true mix of yellow, red, and green.
When breeding an EQA that has no colour type/ bare wings, insert 00. The 0 notation refers to the limiter gene, which like chestnut, is the base for all wing colours. It causes no feather colour to be visible. For example, a bare parent x a red carrying parent would be 00 x RR, and the foal would always develop an 0R genotype. This means that the foal would have no visible feather colour.



FAQ


Q: What does the "n" stand for in a genotype?
A: it stands for a recessive version of the gene. toTO is the same as nTO!
